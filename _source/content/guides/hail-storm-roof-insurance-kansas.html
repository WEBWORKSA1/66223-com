Key takeaways:
  • Take dated "before" photos of your roof and exterior every spring. They're your strongest evidence.
  • Know your deductible type now. Many policies use a percentage-based wind/hail deductible that can be much larger than you expect.
  • After a storm, document everything, prevent further damage, then call your insurer, in that order.
  • Never sign a contract on the spot with a door-knocking contractor, and never pay in full up front.

Why hail matters so much here

Spring and early-summer severe weather, including thunderstorms, hail and tornadoes, is a real part of life in Kansas. For homeowners in 66223, the roof is usually the most exposed and most expensive part of the house to replace, and hail is a leading reason roofs get replaced early. The good news: most of the stress of a hail claim comes from being unprepared, and preparation is cheap.

Before the storm: prepare in late winter

Know your policy

Pull out your homeowners policy (or log in to your insurer's site) and find answers to these questions:

  • What's my wind/hail deductible? Some policies use a flat dollar deductible for all claims. Others use a separate wind/hail deductible set as a percentage of the dwelling coverage amount.
  • Is my roof covered at replacement cost or actual cash value? Replacement cost pays to replace the roof with similar materials. Actual cash value subtracts depreciation for age, which can sharply reduce the payout on an older roof.
  • Are cosmetic damage exclusions in my policy? Some policies exclude dents to metal roofing, gutters or vents that don't affect function.
  • What's the deadline to report a claim? Policies set their own reporting time limits. Find yours and write it down.
  • Does my policy cover code upgrades? If current building code requires something the old roof didn't have, some policies pay for it and some don't.

Build your "before" file

  • Take dated photos and a short video of every side of the house: roof (from the ground or a safe vantage point), gutters, downspouts, siding, windows, screens, the A/C unit, fences and the deck.
  • Save receipts for your roof, gutters and any repairs. Note the roof's installation year and material.
  • Store everything in the cloud, not just on your phone.

During the storm

  • Get people and pets inside, away from windows. If a tornado warning is issued, go to your shelter spot in the lowest interior room.
  • Move cars into the garage if it's safe to do so before the storm arrives.
  • Follow warnings from the National Weather Service and local alerts.
  • Don't go outside to inspect anything until the storm has fully passed.

After the storm: the first 48 hours

  1. Stay safe. Watch for downed power lines and report them to Evergy. Don't climb onto a wet or damaged roof.
  2. Document before you clean. Photograph hailstones next to a coin or ruler, dents on gutters, downspouts, vents and the A/C unit, spatter marks on siding, broken windows and any interior water stains. Date and time matter, so keep the originals.
  3. Prevent further damage. Tarp a damaged roof section or board a broken window if it's safe, or hire someone to. Keep receipts. Most policies expect you to take reasonable steps to limit further damage.
  4. Contact your insurer. Report the claim, get a claim number and ask when an adjuster will inspect.
  5. Keep a claim log. Write down every call: date, name, what was said and next steps.
Tip: Talk to your neighbors. If several houses on your street have damage, that supports your claim, and it can help you compare notes on contractors.

Filing the claim and working with the adjuster

  • Be home for the inspection if you can, and consider having your own roofer there too so they can point out damage.
  • Ask for the adjuster's estimate in writing and review it line by line: roof area, materials, gutters, vents, flashing, siding and interior repairs.
  • Understand payment structure. On replacement-cost policies, insurers often pay actual cash value first and release the remainder after work is finished and documented. Ask how your insurer handles it.
  • If you disagree, ask your insurer about its reinspection and dispute process, and get a second written estimate from a reputable contractor. If you still can't resolve it, the state insurance regulator handles consumer complaints about insurers.
  • Watch for mortgage-company checks. If you have a mortgage, your lender may be named on the claim check and may have its own process for releasing funds.

Avoiding storm-chaser contractor scams

After big hail events, out-of-area crews often arrive and go door to door. Some are legitimate; some aren't. Protect yourself with these rules:

Red flagWhat to do instead
Pressure to sign today, or a "free inspection" that ends with a signatureTake time to get two or three written estimates
Offers to "cover" or "waive" your deductibleWalk away. You're responsible for your deductible, and schemes to avoid it can be insurance fraud
Large cash deposit or full payment up frontPay in stages tied to completed work, by traceable payment
No local address, out-of-state plates only, vague company nameVerify a physical local business presence and references from recent local jobs
Asks you to sign an agreement letting them deal with your insurer for youRead every document carefully; you can stay in control of your own claim
Can't show proof of insuranceRequire certificates of liability and workers' compensation coverage

  • Update your "before" photo file so you're ready for the next season.

Frequently asked questions

Will filing a hail claim raise my premium?

It can, depending on your insurer and claims history, though weather claims are often treated differently from other claim types. Ask your agent directly how a claim would affect your policy before you file, but don't skip reporting legitimate damage.

How do I know if hail damaged my roof?

Look from the ground for dents on gutters, vents and downspouts, granules collecting at downspout outlets, and bruised or cracked shingles. Because roof damage isn't always visible from below, a professional inspection after a significant storm is worth it.

What's the difference between replacement cost and actual cash value?

Replacement cost pays what it costs to replace damaged property with similar new materials. Actual cash value deducts depreciation for age and wear, so an older roof can get a much smaller payout. Your declarations page tells you which applies.

Can a contractor pay my deductible for me?

Treat any offer to waive or absorb your deductible as a serious red flag. The deductible is your contractual responsibility, and arrangements to hide it from your insurer can create legal problems for you. Check with your insurer or an attorney if you have questions.

How long do I have to file a claim?

It depends on your policy. Reporting limits vary by insurer and policy type, so read yours now and file promptly after any storm.
